# Adaptive interview

Read the conversation first. Ask only what is needed to route a credible test;
do not force every person through every question, and never ask a fact already
given. Ask at most seven short questions total. In an interactive conversation,
ask one question, use its answer, then ask the next best question.

## Starting reality

- What are you doing now: starting from zero, operating an offer, or improving
  an existing business?
- What result do you need first, and by when: a paid trial, a deposit, a signed
  agreement, or a settled payment?
- For an experienced operator, ask first: What is the existing offer, its basic
  economics, the bottleneck, and the highest-leverage point you can change?

## Assets and access

- What skills, work samples, tools, customer relationships, local access, or
  distribution do you already control?
- How many hours and how much cash can you risk on this test?
- What can you personally approve, and what requires another person's approval?

## Buyer and proof

- Who has the problem, what evidence says they have it, and how can they be
  reached with permission?
- What offer, price, sales history, outcomes, testimonials, or other proof is
  real today? Label missing proof as unknown, not as a negative or a promise.

## Constraints

- What geography, licensing, employer, contract, privacy, consent, platform,
  or regulatory rules apply?
- What delivery capacity, quality standard, and fulfillment boundary can you
  commit to only after approval?

## Speed and ambition

- What is the fastest acceptable evidence of demand, and what cost ceiling
  would make the experiment not worth continuing?
- Is the goal to repair an existing cash engine, sell a service, grow an owned
  audience, or explore a new capability?

## Questions to skip when already answered

Skip known stage, assets, time, budget, audience, offer, proof, geography,
constraints, and permissions. Do not re-ask facts in a persona or earlier
conversation. When the user has an existing cash engine, do not start with
generic idea generation: clarify its offer, economics, bottleneck, and leverage
point first. When a beginner lacks business vocabulary, use concrete examples
such as "a spreadsheet cleanup for three local shops" or "a fixed-price editing
trial"; examples are prompts, not claims about their skills or demand.

## Fast interview output

After enough facts are available, stop interviewing and produce:

1. a Revenue Profile separating known facts, assumptions, and open questions;
2. a Route Decision scoring no more than three choices, with one route and one
   fallback; and
3. an Experiment Brief for a 24-hour, human-approved payable test.

If a material constraint is unknown, show a safe discovery action and downgrade
the certainty instead of filling the gap with an assumption.
